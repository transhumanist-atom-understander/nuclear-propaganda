There have always been three main challenges to the viability of nuclear power: over-regulation, competition from natural gas, and stagnant electricity demand.
It is easy to list them, but hard to rank their importance.

Let's start with over-regulation.
The poster child of nuclear over-regulation was the US standard that radiation should be "as low as reasonably achievable" (ALARA), which might sound nice but [was bad](https://www.lesswrong.com/s/cuwFMDLAKguxmGkGY/p/ThvvCE2HsLohJYd7b#ALARA).
Perhaps more important than such standards, which are at least predictable, was local interference with construction.
As a dramatic example, [a nuclear plant was fully completed on Long Island but never started due to local opposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoreham_Nuclear_Power_Plant).

In the '00s, a "nuclear renaissance" seemed to be in the works.
In the US, [fourteen nuclear reactors were licensed for construction](https://www.nrc.gov/licensing-applications/new-reactor-licensing/large-light-water-reactors/combined-license-holders-for-new-reactors).
[Only two were built, supported heavily by government loans](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Vogtle_Electric_Generating_Plant&oldid=1382708962#Units_3_and_4).
[The Obama administration had planned further support for nuclear power, but this was considered politically difficult after the Fukushima disaster](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2011-03-14/obama-s-nuclear-power-expansion-plans-set-back-by-japanese-quake-aftermath).
The worst overreaction to the Fukushima disaster, however, was in Germany, and by now all German nuclear power plants have been shut down.

I expect LessWrong users to know that story.
I'm going to tell two others.

As nuclear developed, it also faced increasing competition from natural gas plants.
Unlike nuclear or coal plants, natural gas turbines are cheap to construct.
Early investment in nuclear coincided with fears of shortages of natural resources.
[A nuclear exec in 1970](https://www.nytimes.com/1970/11/29/archives/nuclear-power-arrives-again-utilities-1970-orders-of-aplants-soar.html): "Let’s face it, electric utilities are now faced with gas that will become absolutely unavailable in the long term".
That didn't happen.

But where I hear about gas most often is not with reference to the original wave of nuclear power construction in the '70s and '80s, but the cancellation of the '00s nuclear renaissance.
At least in the United States, this is often blamed on the fracking revolution (for example, in [The Economist](https://www.economist.com/united-states/2013/06/01/fracked-off)).

But to me, it seems like the '08 financial crisis hit harder than abundant natural gas.
Not primarily because it made nuclear power difficult to finance, but because the economic slowdown stopped electricity demand growth.
Not that US generation had been growing quickly before that, but [after '08 it was flat](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/electricity-mix?tab=line&country=%7EUSA&source=total&metric=generation&frequency=annual).
To imagine what might have been, consider that Tesla's first car was introduced in '08, the beginning of [a historic dip in car purchases](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/totalsa).
Nobody buying electric cars or electricity-using appliances, no electricity demand growth.
No forecasted demand growth, no business case for nuclear power.

But stagnant electricity demand also limited the original nuclear buildout of the '70s and '80s.
You can see it most clearly in France.
There is no mystery as to why the French stopped constructing nuclear reactors: they were not fully using the ones they had.
[In 1988 they were running at 61% of full capacity, on average](https://www.fundinguniverse.com/company-histories/electricit%C3%A9-de-france-history/).

Those are our three stories: over-regulation, competition with natural gas, and stagnant demand.
As I said, easy to list, hard to rank.

In the tech enthusiast blogosphere, I hear mainly about over-regulation.
I read about the others from [Vaclav Smil](https://www.google.com/books/edition/Invention_and_Innovation/yJZuEAAAQBAJ?hl=en), or articles in mainstream publications.

The dreams of the atomic space age: electricity too cheap to meter, desalination making the deserts bloom.
The story in the tech enthusiast blogosphere is that these dreams could have come true.
Would have come true, had it not been for the anti-nuclear movement.

Well, maybe so.
But maybe not.
It depends on the relative importance of over-regulation, competition with natural gas, and stagnant electricity demand.
